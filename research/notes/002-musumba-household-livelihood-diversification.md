# Research Note 002: Household Livelihood Diversification in Rural Africa

**Authors:** Mark Musumba, Cheryl A. Palm, Adam M. Komarek, Patrick K. Mutuo and Bocary Kaya  
**Publication:** *Agricultural Economics*, 53(2), 246–256 (2022)  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/agec.12694  
**Research series:** Informal Economy, Livelihoods and Economic Resilience  
**Document type:** Empirical literature analysis and product-research translation  
**Status:** Analysis of published research; not original empirical research by Asili Tech.

## 1. Purpose

This note examines how Musumba and colleagues measured livelihood diversification among rural households in sub-Saharan Africa, what factors were associated with differences in livelihood portfolios, and what the study suggests—and does not establish—for Asili Tech's research into informal work and economic resilience.

The study is evidence about rural agricultural households in eight selected Millennium Villages. It is not a representative survey of all African households, urban informal workers or South African micro-businesses.

## 2. Study at a glance

The authors used two waves of household survey data, from 2009 and 2011, covering 1,773 households in eight villages across eight sub-Saharan African countries. The balanced panel contained 3,546 household-year observations.

The countries were Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal, Mali, Rwanda, Tanzania, Malawi and Uganda. The study considered three broad livelihood activity categories:

- Crop production
- Livestock activities
- Non-farm activities, including temporary non-farm employment, business enterprises and salaried employment

The surveys covered a range of household, farm, livelihood, income, expenditure, asset, credit, transfer and shock information. The authors selected these survey years because they were the years with comparable questions on non-farm activities across the eight villages.

**Source:** Musumba et al. (2022), Sections 2.1–2.3. See the [publisher's full open-access article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/agec.12694) and [CGIAR repository record](https://cgspace.cgiar.org/items/7fb2cb8a-aad0-4b82-b82d-b8a56bd854a7).

## 3. Conceptual framework

The study was informed by the Sustainable Rural Livelihoods framework. It considers five types of capital:

1. Human: education and labour.
2. Natural: land and water.
3. Financial: savings and credit.
4. Physical: roads and infrastructure.
5. Social: informal safety nets and organisational membership.

The framework also considers the vulnerability context, including seasonality and shocks such as weather and output-price variability. The authors use this framework to organise possible drivers of livelihood strategy choices.

## 4. How the study measured diversification

The main livelihood diversity index was a count of the number of broad livelihood activities in which a household participated:

`LD_i = Crop_i + Livestock_i + NonFarm_i`

Each component represents involvement in that activity category. The index therefore ranges from 1 to 3 in the study's analytic sample.

This is a measure of breadth, not a measure of income shares, profitability, risk-adjusted returns or the balance between activities. A household with 90% of its income from crops and 10% from non-farm activity can receive the same count score as one with a 50/50 split.

The authors also constructed a non-farm index based on the count of non-farm activity types (temporary employment, business enterprises and salaried employment), ranging from 0 to 3. They used a crop index based on the squared ratio of the number of crops grown by the household to the maximum number of crops grown by households in its village.

## 5. Methods

The analysis combined:

- Exploratory descriptive statistics to describe livelihood activities and combinations.
- A correlated random-effects model to examine factors associated with livelihood diversity.
- Transition probabilities to examine changes in household livelihood portfolios between 2009 and 2011.
- Inverse probability weights to adjust for possible bias associated with sample attrition.

The article reports a 24% attrition rate across all villages and describes the adjustment used to test and address attrition concerns. The authors explicitly state that their purpose was not to evaluate the impact of the Millennium Villages project: all sampled households were exposed to project investments.

A simplified representation of the count outcome is:

`LD_it ∈ {1, 2, 3}`

The correlated random-effects approach relates the outcome to observed explanatory variables while accounting for household-level unobserved heterogeneity under the model's assumptions. This is not equivalent to a randomised experiment, and estimated associations should not be interpreted as causal effects.

## 6. Main findings reported by the authors

### 6.1 Diversification was common, but heterogeneous

The authors report that 40% of households conducted all three broad livelihood activities: crop, livestock and non-farm. Non-farm activities were reported by 50% of households, although the types of non-farm activities varied by location.

The most common combinations, in rank order across villages, were all three activities; crop and livestock; crop only; and crop and non-farm.

### 6.2 Non-farm participation increased

The share of households reporting non-farm activities increased by 7% between the 2009 and 2011 survey waves, the largest increase among the three broad activity categories reported by the authors. This is the paper's reported percentage change; it should not be silently re-labelled as a seven-percentage-point increase.

### 6.3 Livelihood portfolios changed over time

Nineteen percent of households were classified as having a transient livelihood: they moved from one activity to multiple activities, or from multiple activities to one, between the two survey waves.

The rate varied substantially by village: 43% in Bonsaaso, Ghana; 35% in Mayange, Rwanda; 31% in Mbola, Tanzania; 19% in Mwandama, Malawi; 15% in Pampaida, Nigeria; 9% in Ruhiira, Uganda; 6% in Tiby, Mali; and 4% in Potou, Senegal.

This variation matters. An average across eight villages can conceal very different local livelihood dynamics.

### 6.4 Factors associated with livelihood diversity

The authors report that larger cultivated land area and access to credit were important drivers associated with livelihood diversity. Their conclusion highlights non-agricultural credit as positively associated with livelihood diversity and drought as negatively associated with it. The paper also reports that crop land area had a positive association with the number of livelihood activities.

These are model-based associations in the studied sample. They do not establish that providing credit will always cause diversification, or that drought has the same effect in every setting.

## 7. Visual interpretation: what the measures capture

The study's count index captures the number of broad categories represented in a household's livelihood portfolio. It does not capture the financial weight of each activity.

Consider two illustrative households (these are examples, not observations from the paper):

| Illustrative household | Activity A share | Activity B share | Activity count |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Household A | 90% | 10% | 2 |
| Household B | 50% | 50% | 2 |

Both have two activities, but their income concentration differs. The count index alone cannot distinguish them.

To understand the financial structure, a future study might need income shares, costs, net contributions, time allocations, volatility and co-movement between income streams. These would be additional measures, not measures reported in this paper.

## 8. Critical assessment and limitations

1. **Breadth is not balance.** A count of activities does not reveal how much income each contributes.
2. **Activity participation is not profitability.** The index does not subtract costs or value unpaid labour.
3. **Two survey waves provide a limited time window.** The study captures changes between 2009 and 2011, not the full dynamics of longer-term livelihoods.
4. **Context matters.** The eight selected rural villages differ in farming systems, rainfall and local opportunities. Their results should not be generalised uncritically to urban South Africa.
5. **Associations are not causal effects.** The regression design does not establish that a policy intervention will produce a particular outcome.
6. **The study was not an impact evaluation.** The authors explicitly distinguish their analysis from evaluation of the wider Millennium Villages project.

## 9. What this may mean for Asili Tech

The following are Asili Tech interpretations and hypotheses, not claims made or tested by the authors.

### 9.1 AMKA: move beyond counting income sources

The paper shows why the number of activities is a useful starting point but an incomplete description of a financial life. AMKA could investigate whether users benefit from activity-level records that distinguish:

- Revenue and costs by activity.
- Net contribution after relevant costs.
- Share of total income attributable to each activity.
- Seasonality and income volatility.
- Time and working-capital requirements.
- Whether income streams are exposed to similar shocks.

**Hypothesis AMKA-02:** Activity-level financial records and measures of income concentration may help some users understand how their income sources contribute to available cash and how exposed they are to changes in individual activities.

This is not yet validated. It requires discovery research on how users define income, what they can realistically record, whether summaries are understood and whether they inform decisions.

### 9.2 SkillVault: represent changing work portfolios

The study's transition analysis raises a question about professional identity: work portfolios can change. A worker may move between services, temporary employment, trading and other activities over time.

SkillVault could investigate how profiles represent multiple skills and work histories without reducing a person to one job title. Evidence should remain verifiable, understandable and fair to new entrants.

**Hypothesis SKILLVAULT-02:** A flexible profile that records evidence across different service categories may represent informal workers' changing work histories more accurately than a single occupation label.

This is not validated. Customer trust, profile usability, verification fairness and effects on access to work require testing.

## 10. Questions for Asili Tech's research agenda

1. Does the number of income sources tell us enough about economic resilience?
2. How should diversification be measured when activities have different units, costs and income patterns?
3. Can income concentration and volatility distinguish more balanced portfolios from portfolios dominated by one activity?
4. How do shocks affect the ability to maintain multiple income sources?
5. How do workers themselves distinguish opportunity-led diversification from diversification driven by necessity?
6. What does livelihood diversification look like in South African urban informal businesses, including trading, services, casual employment and gig work?
7. What information can people realistically record without imposing an excessive administrative burden?

## 11. Proposed next steps

1. Review current South African and regional evidence before applying the rural findings to urban informal work.
2. Conduct appropriately approved discovery interviews to understand users' income definitions, activities, costs and record-keeping practices.
3. Test simple activity-level financial summaries and ask users to explain what they mean.
4. Compare count-based measures with income-share, concentration and volatility measures where reliable data and consent exist.
5. Investigate whether flexible professional profiles better represent workers' skills and work transitions.
6. Evaluate usability, data burden, privacy, fairness and unintended consequences before making product claims.

Any research involving people or their information must follow applicable legal, institutional and ethical requirements. Ordinary product data should not automatically be treated as research data.

## 12. Research takeaway

**Counting activities tells us how many livelihood categories a household participates in; it does not tell us how financially important, profitable or risky those activities are.**

For Asili Tech, this suggests a useful next research step: investigate the quality and structure of income portfolios, not just their size. AMKA may help users understand the financial composition of their activities, while SkillVault may need to represent the changing composition of their work. Both propositions remain hypotheses to test.
